## Abstract

Large Language Models (LLMs) have displayed remarkable capabilities across diverse domains, but their training remains resource- and time-intensive, requiring massive computational resources and careful orchestration of training procedures. Model souping-the practice of averaging weights from multiple models of the same architecture-has emerged as a promising pre- and post-training technique that can enhance performance without expensive retraining. We observe that previous souping approaches can lead to collapse in precision-sensitive LLM capabilities. In this paper, we introduce SoCE, a principled approach for model souping to overcome this shortcoming. The proposed method utilizes benchmark composition to identify optimal model candidates and applies non-uniform weighted averaging to maximize performance. Contrary to previous approaches, our method leverages the observation that different clusters (or categories) of points within a benchmark often exhibit low inter-correlations in model performance. SoCE identifies "expert" models for each weakly-correlated category cluster and combines them using optimized weighted averaging rather than uniform weights. We demonstrate that SoCE improves performance and robustness across multiple domains and achieves state-of-the-art results on the Berkeley Function Calling Leaderboard.

## Contributions (from the paper)

Recent advances in model souping—averaging weights from multiple models of the same architecture—have shown promising results as a post-training technique to enhance performance without requiring expensive retraining. Prior work has demonstrated that uniform averaging of model weights can lead to improved performance across various tasks Wortsman et al. (2022a) and mitigate issues like catastrophic forgetting Kleiman et al. (2025). These findings suggest that model souping offers a computationally efficient alternative to traditional approaches that rely on modifying training data recipes or retraining procedures. Recently, the concept of "model souping" has evolved into a broader model merging paradigm that combines the weights of multiple task-specific derivative models into a single, highly capable network without the need for additional training. TIES-Merging (Yadav et al., 2023), an example of such an approach, isolates the fine-tuning updates and resets up to 80% of the smallest parameter changes to zero, retaining only the most significant delta weights. DARE (Yu et al., 2024) proposes randomly dropping a vast majority of these fine-tuned delta weights and rescaling the remaining parameters to maintain their overall mathematical expectation. While these approaches have been shown to push performance, our experiments show that these approaches can collapse several LLM capabilities which require exact instruction adherence, such as tool-use, long-context, and reasoning (Tables 3, 6 and 8). ## Conclusion (from the paper)

This work introduces SoCE, a category-aware, principled model souping technique that achieves state-of-the-art results and performance improvement across diverse domains. We demonstrate that this approach not only improves overall benchmark performance but also enhances consistency and robustness across categories. We hope these findings inspire further research into efficient model aggregation and reuse within the community. We believe that the presented method can propose a new perspective for the OSS community to reuse and revive derivative models of the same architecture.

## Limitations (from the paper)

The results presented in this paper advance our understanding of model souping, but some methodological and conceptual limitations warrant more discussion. Benchmark structure: Our method assumes that the target benchmark ships with an explicit sub-categorical decomposition and that each sub-category contains enough data points for per-category scores to be estimated reliably across a sufficient number of models. All experiments in this paper use benchmarks with pre-defined category labels. Many benchmarks do not expose such structure, and we leave the discovery of implicit benchmark structure (for example, by means of clustering) to future work.
